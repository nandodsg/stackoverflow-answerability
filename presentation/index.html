    { 02 · the problem }

    The problem we are solving

    What makes a Stack Overflow question get answered, and get an accepted answer? Three questions on the public dataset, answered with a dbt project, guarded by tests written from acceptance criteria, and computed again on this page by SQL you can edit.

    1. 1 · tags

      What tags on a Stack Overflow question lead to the most answers and the highest rate of approved answers for the current year? What tags lead to the least? How about combinations of tags?

    2. 2 · python vs dbt

      For posts which are tagged with only ‘python’ or ‘dbt’, what is the year over year change of question-to-answer ratio for the last 10 years? How about the rate of approved answers on questions for the same time period? How do posts tagged with only ‘python’ compare to posts only tagged with ‘dbt’?

    3. 3 · beyond tags

      Other than tags, what qualities on a post correlate with the highest rate of answer and approved answer?

    { 03 · the data }

    Frozen at 2022-09-25, and full of traps

    A snapshot that stopped on 2022-09-25 at 05:56 UTC, with 23,020,127 questions since 2008, so "this year" means 2022, a partial year. All three prompts rank, one way or another, answers against questions (answers per question, answer rate, accepted rate), so knowing what makes that ratio spin is the key point. Four things in this data can spin it without the question being any better or worse. Here they are as problems; the next slide is what was done about them.

    • 1 · Small groups

      2

      questions at most behind every tag in the raw 2022 top and bottom 10, all at exactly 100% or 0% accepted. The median tag has 6 questions, so one more accepted answer moves its accepted rate by 17 points. An accepted rate from a handful of questions is mostly luck. Ranked raw, P1 would just list the luckiest and unluckiest tiny tags.

    • 2 · Edits and leakage

      45%

      of 2022 questions were edited at some point: 9% had their title or body first edited after the first answer, 4% their tags. The table keeps only the latest version, so the answer can leave its mark on the question: someone answers with dplyr, then adds the dplyr tag. That is leakage, the outcome seeping into what should explain it.

    • 3 · Censoring

      73.5%

      answer rate of 2022 python-only questions, down from 84.8% in 2021. Most of that is not a collapse: a question from September 2022 had days to be answered, not months. That is censoring, an outcome cut off before it had time to happen, and it makes the newest year look the worst.

    • 4 · Survivorship

      up

      is the direction of the bias. Only questions that survived are in the data, and Stack Overflow deletes mostly unanswered, low-score ones, so every rate reads high, and older years (more time to be cleaned up) more. This data can't say by how much.

    The good news: the keys are clean. answer_count matches the joined answers for every question, no answer is orphaned, and only 292 of 11.8M accepted-answer ids (all from 2008–2012) don't resolve; a warning test keeps listing them.

    { 04 · decisions: how to deal with our data's traps }

    One answer per trap

    Traps 1 and 2 had a setting to choose, and an EDA settled each one before any dbt model. Traps 3 and 4 have none: one is checked, the other stated as a limit.

    1. D1 · trap 1, small groups

      How do we keep luck from ranking small groups?

      Ranked by raw accepted rate, tiny tags land at 100% or 0% by sheer luck. Each question is a yes/no draw (like a coin flip), so a tag's accepted rate measured again lands within ±1.96 standard errors of its true accepted rate 95% of the time, and that band narrows as the tag grows. How many questions until it is ±5 points at worst?

      Decided: a 385-question floor, (1.96 × 0.5 / 0.05)² = 384.2; lists sort by the Wilson bound, not the raw accepted rate.

      Same floor at 99% or 99.7%: the lists barely move.

    2. D4 · trap 2, edits and leakage

      Which version of an edited question do we measure?

      Only today's version is kept, and 9% of 2022 questions had their title or body first edited after the first answer. Dropping edited questions is biased: they do better (accepted rate 34% vs 28%). The original is in post_history, one 106 GiB read away.

      Decided: P3 reads the text as first posted; P1 and P2 keep today's tags, as the prompts ask, and are rebuilt on the original ones; every row shows its exposure, the share of its questions first edited after the first answer.

      Retagging made the #1 tag pair; single tags hold.

    3. Trap 3, censoring

      Is the 2022 drop real?

      Mostly not. From 2021 to 2022 the answer rate fell from 84.8% to 73.5%, while answers per answered question barely moved (1.80 → 1.71): about three quarters of the drop is questions not answered yet when the data stops.

      Checked: every list is rebuilt counting only answers within 30 days, on questions that had those 30 days; P2 flags 2022 as partial, and P3 makes no claim on it.

      30-day lists: 9 of the top 10 and 6 of the bottom 10 tags stay.

    4. Trap 4, survivorship

      What about deleted questions?

      They are not in the data, and Stack Overflow deletes mostly unanswered, low-score ones. Every rate reads high, and older years, with more time to be cleaned up, read higher.

      Stated as a limit: the direction is known, the size is not. The real gap between top and bottom tags is likely a bit wider, and P2's long decline a bit less steep, than shown.

      This snapshot has no trace of what was deleted.

    Every decision and its why: the decisions log in PLAN.md.

    D1 · EDA

    How the floor was measured

    Question
    How many questions before a tag's accepted rate is mostly signal, not luck?
    Data
    2022 questions from posts_questions, metadata only (tags, dates, answer_count, accepted answer id), never title or body. One row per tag: 42,627 tags; tag pairs as a frequency table (934K pairs).
    Method
    Each question either gets an accepted answer or doesn't, like a coin flip weighted by the tag's true accepted rate. With few questions, luck can push the measured accepted rate far from the true one; the standard error, √(p(1 − p) / n), measures how far, and it shrinks as the tag gets more questions. A simulation checks it: 100,000 runs of a 385-question tag (385 weighted coin flips each) put the measured accepted rate within ±1.96 standard errors of the true one 94.8% of the time, against the 95% the formula promises. The funnel plot then asks whether a tag's true accepted rate is above or below the all-tag 30.2%. It shows, for every tag size, where a tag whose true accepted rate is 30.2% would land by luck alone: inside the dashed lines 95% of the time, inside the dotted lines 99.8%. So even if every tag's true accepted rate were exactly 30.2%, about 5% of tags would fall outside the dashed lines just by luck. Under 30 questions, 6.7% do: only 1.7 points more than luck alone. For almost none of these tags can we tell a true accepted rate above or below 30.2% from a 30.2% tag that got lucky or unlucky. The share outside grows with size (38% at 30–99 questions, 60% at 100–499, 80% at 500–1,999). With 2,000+ questions, 82% fall outside even the dotted lines, where luck explains only 0.2%: their true accepted rates really are above or below 30.2%. The floor follows from a target: ±5 points at worst, 95% of the time. Luck moves an accepted rate most when it is 50%, so the smallest n with 1.96 × √(0.5 × 0.5 / n) ≤ 0.05 is (1.96 × 0.5 / 0.05)² = 384.2, rounded up to 385, the textbook poll size. The lists then rank by the Wilson bound, each tag's 95% range for its true accepted rate: highest lists sort by its low end, lowest lists by its high end. Is 95% too lax? Ranking the same tags with 99% or 99.7% ranges keeps all of the top 10 and 9 and 8 of the bottom 10. The floor is what moves the lists: a 99% target needs 664 questions, and at 664, 9 of the bottom 10 drop out, because they have fewer (median 518). Two other ways of ranking the same tags agree: the raw accepted rate and empirical Bayes (each accepted rate pulled toward 30.2%, harder the fewer questions the tag has) share 9 or 10 names with every top and bottom 10.
    Impact
    P1: 1,162 of 42,627 tags ranked in 2022 (2.7% of tags, but 76% of question–tag links), plus 1,026 tag pairs. P2: nothing is ranked, so no floor; the same ±1.96 standard-error intervals show the dbt years overlap, so no dbt trend is claimed. P3: groups under 385 questions are not reported.
    Trade-offs
    • Small tags are left out, even good ones. A tag needs 385 questions in the year to be ranked. google-query-language had 277 questions in 2022 and 89.9% of them got an accepted answer, more than any ranked tag, but it is under the floor, so it doesn't appear. The lists answer "which tags do best among those measured to ±5 points", not "which tags do best".
    • The bottom 10 depends on where the floor sits. The tags with the lowest accepted rates tend to be smaller: 9 of the bottom 10 have fewer than 664 questions (median 518). Raising the floor to 664, the 99% version of the ±5-point target, would drop those 9 and bring in 9 others. Changing only the confidence level, with the floor kept at 385, barely moves the lists (the top 10 stays the same, the bottom 10 keeps 8 or 9). So the floor, not the confidence level, decides who is in the bottom 10.
    • A tag can leave the list just by shrinking. The floor is 385 every year. purrr and stringr were #1 and #3 in 2021; in 2022 they had 368 and 270 questions, so they aren't ranked, even though 68.5% and 69.3% of their questions got an accepted answer, above every ranked 2022 tag. Comparing the lists across years means checking whether a tag left because of its accepted rate or because of its size.
    • About 1 range in 20 is wrong. Each tag's 95% range holds its true accepted rate 95% of the time, so across 1,162 ranked tags about 58 ranges miss, and we can't tell which. A tag at the edge of the top 10 could truly belong just outside it. That's why the lists were also built two other ways (raw accepted rate and empirical Bayes): they share 9 or 10 of every 10 names, so the lists hold even if a single position might swap.
    Tools
    BigQuery SQL: 3 queries in run_eda.py, about 3.4 GB in total, each dry-run first and capped in bytes; results cached as CSV. Everything after that reads the cache, never BigQuery: floor_precision.py (simulation, floors, tag sizes, tags left out) and presentation/eda_charts.py (the charts in this dialog). Python with pandas, NumPy and matplotlib.

    Executive impact of the decision

    “Why is P1 ruling out tags with fewer than 385 questions?”

    Because below that size, a tag's accepted rate is mostly luck. If we rank all tags raw, the whole top 10 and bottom 10 are tags with one or two questions, at exactly 100% or 0%. Half of all tags have six questions or fewer, and at six, a single accepted answer moves the accepted rate by 17 points.

    385 is the same sample size a political poll uses: it's the smallest number of questions where a tag's accepted rate is right to within ±5 points, 95% of the time.

    What it costs: we rank only 1,162 of 42,627 tags, but those cover 76% of everything asked in 2022.

    • “So we ignore small tags?” We can't rank them reliably. If one matters to the business, we can track it over several years, or group it with related tags until it reaches 385 questions.
    • “Why ±5 and 95%?” It's the standard polling target. We also tested stricter settings (99% and 99.7% confidence): the top 10 didn't change, and the bottom 10 kept 8 or 9 of its tags.
    • “Is 385 the right number?” A stricter target (±5 points at 99%) needs 664 questions. That mostly changes the bottom 10, because the tags with the lowest accepted rates also tend to be smaller.

    D4 · EDA

    How edits and leakage were measured

    live SQL · DuckDB-WASM · P1's result table, with its exposure columnloading engine…

    The bars are each tag's exposure. For the bottom 10, order by accepted_rate_upper ascending (D1 ranks the lowest by the upper bound): all at or under 3.1%. Order by tag_exposure DESC to find webdriverwait.

    Question
    How often are questions edited, does an edit come after the answer, is a never-edited filter safe, and do edits change the three answers?
    Data
    post_history for 2022 questions, and for python-only and dbt-only questions of 2013–2022: the kind of each edit (title, body or tags, edited or rolled back), its time and its editor, never its text column; joined to the time of each question's first answer. For P1, the 1,162 tags with 385+ questions (D1).
    Method
    The table keeps only today's title, body and tags. post_history logs every revision with its kind and time, so each question's first edit of each kind can be set against its first answer, the earliest moment an answer can shape an edit (when an answer was accepted is not in the data). Any editor counts, the asker included. In 2022, 45% of questions were edited: 13.5% their tags, 40% their title or body. For 4.4% the first tag edit, and for 8.9% the first title or body edit, came after the first answer. Why not keep only never-edited questions? Edited questions do better (answer rate 67% vs 57%, accepted rate 34% vs 28%), so that filter would drop 45% of the year and keep its weaker part. The gap isn't even stable: among python-only questions, the edited ones had the lower accepted rate every year from 2013 to 2020 (62.7% vs 68.3% in 2013). The risk is measured as exposure: the share of a group's questions whose relevant field was first edited after the first answer (tags for P1 and P2, title or body for P3). Across the 1,162 ranked tags, tag exposure has a median of 3.7%, 1 tag in 10 is above 10%, and webdriverwait reaches 62%. It rises with the accepted rate: a rank correlation of 0.67 (sort the tags by each measure and compare the two orders; 1 is the same order, 0 no relation). Part of that is built in, since an unanswered question can't be edited after an answer, but counting only answered questions it is still 0.49. So the possible leakage sits where P1's headline is. Exposure says what could leak, not whether it moved anything, so each answer is also rebuilt on the version the asker first posted (the original tags for P1 and P2, the original title and body for P3, all from one read of post_history.text), and a warn test lists every result that moves. What they found is under Impact.
    Impact
    • P1. Every row carries its tag exposure. Rebuilt on the original tags, a warn test flags 46 tags and 65 pairs (a top or bottom 10 changes, or an accepted rate moves more than 2.5 points, half of D1's ±5-point target). 9 of 10 tags stay in each accepted-rate list: tidyr (#5) falls under 385 questions on its original tags and java-stream comes in; at the bottom, usb gives way to video-streaming. Pairs keep 7 of the top 10 and 8 of the bottom 10. The #1 pair, arrays + google-sheets (exposure 64%), falls under 385: it is mostly made by retagging after an answer, so it isn't quoted as a finding.
    • P2. Tag exposure is 3.6–5.1% of python-only questions a year and 0–3.2% of dbt-only ones. The original-tags check flags 3 rows (python 2019 and 2021, dbt 2022), none of which changes a conclusion, and dropping every question whose tags were ever edited moves python 2022 from 1.26 to 1.25 answers per question.
    • P3. Every feature is measured on the text as first posted. Today's text would have given 5.1–7.4% of each year's questions a different "has a code block" value (beyond the format noise between the two texts), yet the effects barely move (third chart), and the warn test raises one warning: five or more tags, on the accepted rate among answered questions (−0.3 points on the original version, +1.5 on today's).
    Trade-offs
    • P1 and P2 still carry some leakage. The prompts ask how a question is tagged, and today's tags are what a reader sees, so the lists keep them; the original-tags rebuild says where that matters. tidyr is #5 on today's tags, but on the tags askers first chose it has fewer than 385 questions (exposure 14.6%): part of the tag was added after answers.
    • Exposure is a lower bound. It counts a question only when the first edit of that kind came after the first answer, so a question whose tags were edited before and again after its first answer counts as not exposed. The rebuild compares the original version with today's, so it catches those; the exposure column alone doesn't.
    • Exposure is partly answer rate. An unanswered question can't be exposed, so tags whose questions get answered score higher: the 0.67 rank correlation with the accepted rate drops to 0.49 counting only answered questions.
    • The line is the first answer, not the accepted one. When an answer was accepted isn't in the data, so an edit after the first answer but before the accepted one counts as exposed, and an edit prompted by a comment before any answer doesn't (it can't be the answer's doing).
    • The original text costs a 106 GiB read. On a free tier of 1 TB a month, that read became a decision of its own: how it was priced, proved worth it, run once and guarded while an AI agent wrote the queries is D5, on slide 5.
    • Never-edited is a check, not the answer. It would drop 45% of questions, and the better part of them, so it is one of P3's sensitivity versions (a code block: +12.0 on never-edited questions, +11.8 on the original text) rather than a filter.
    Tools
    BigQuery SQL: 2 queries in run_eda.py (6.9 GB and 5.2 GB), each dry-run first and capped at 10 GB, results cached as CSV; they read revision metadata (ids, kinds, times, editors), never the text column. The checks under Impact run in dbt: exposure in int_question_exposure (metadata only), the original versions from the one-time post_history.text read (106 GiB; its budget is D5), and three warn tests. The charts in this dialog come from presentation/eda_charts.py, which reads the cached CSVs and the exported P3 mart, never BigQuery. Python with pandas and matplotlib.

    Executive impact of the decision

    “Can we trust the top tags, if tags get added after the answer?”

    Yes, and we checked rather than assumed. Stack Overflow keeps only today's version of a question, and 9% of 2022 questions had their text changed after someone answered (4% their tags), sometimes by the person answering. Left alone, the lists would credit tags that answerers add: webdriverwait has 62% of its questions retagged after an answer.

    So every list is rebuilt on the tags the asker first chose. 9 of the top 10 and 9 of the bottom 10 tags stay. The top tag pair doesn't (most of it is retagging), so we don't quote it. P3, which is about the question itself, reads the text as first posted.

    What it costs: one 106 GiB read of the revision history, planned against a free monthly budget (D5).

    • “Why not just drop the edited questions?” Almost half the questions were edited, and they don't behave like the rest (in 2022 they do better: accepted rate 34% vs 28%), so dropping them would bias every rate.
    • “Why not rank on the original tags, then?” The prompt asks how questions are tagged, and today's tags are what people see and search by. The rebuild shows the answer barely changes either way.
    • “How do we know the answer caused the edit?” For any single question, we don't. Exposure counts what could have leaked; the rebuild measures how much the results actually move, and that's what we report.

    { 05 · decisions: definitions and budget }

    What the prompts left open, and what it cost

    Two phrases in the prompts can be read two ways, and one column of the data costs a tenth of the free monthly quota to read. Each was measured before choosing.

    1. D2 · definition, P1 and P2

      Accepted out of what?

      "Rate of approved answers" has two readings: out of all questions, or out of the answered ones. The top 10 barely cares (8 of 10 tags shared). The bottom 10 does (5 of 10), because the worst tags are the ones nobody answers.

      Decided: all questions as the headline, with answer rate and accepted-among-answered beside it on every row; the two multiply back to it.

      magento2: 41% answered, 9% accepted.

    2. D3 · definition, P2

      Which way up is the ratio?

      Questions per answer and answers per question carry the same information, but not the same headline: python-only 2021 → 2022 is −17% one way and +21% the other.

      Decided: answers per question: higher reads as more answers, and P1 uses the same metric. Questions per answer is its inverse (1 ÷ answers per question), shown once.

      answer_count matches the joined answers exactly.

    3. D5 · budget, P1–P3

      We're on a free tier: how do we not overspend?

      The project runs in BigQuery's free sandbox: 1 TB of queries a month, 10 GB of storage. D4 needs the original text, and one read of post_history.text bills 106 GiB whatever the filter: BigQuery bills whole columns, and this table has no partition. And an AI coding agent writes and runs the queries.

      Decided: price every query before it runs; prove the read is worth it on metadata first (12 GB); read once, on its own dbt target capped at 120 GiB, keeping all that later steps need; cap everything else at 20 GiB. The agent works inside caps, tests and a gate that asks a person before it spends.

      511 GiB billed in all: 45% of October's free quota, US$ 0.

    D5 · EDA

    How the budget was planned, guarded and spent

    Question
    Can a free sandbox pay for the original text D4 needs, and how do we keep an AI coding agent from spending the month's quota by accident?
    Data
    BigQuery's own job log for the project (INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS_BY_PROJECT): 685 query jobs from October 29 to November 1, with the bytes each one billed and, for dbt's queries, the target and the model (dbt writes both into every query it sends); never the query text. Plus the free dry runs that priced each big read before it ran.
    Method
    The sandbox gives 1 TB of queries a month and 10 GB of storage, at no cost; past the quota, queries stop until the next month. A dry run is free and returns what a query would bill, so the two text columns were priced first: post_history.text 109 GB and posts_questions.body 36 GB (the models that read them, with a few more columns, billed 106 GiB and 35 GiB). A filter doesn't make them cheaper: BigQuery bills every column a query touches, in full, and these tables have no partition, so keeping only the three revision kinds D4 needs still reads all 106 GiB. Most other queries read 1–7 GB. Then, in order. (1) Prove the read is worth it with metadata: D4's two EDA queries (6.9 + 5.2 GB, about a tenth of the read) showed edits are common, biased, and sit where P1's headline is. (2) Design it once, in the spec, before paying: one read with its size stated (acceptance criterion AC-3.4), storing the features, the original titles and tags (P1 and P2 need them too) and a 5% sample of the raw text, about 3 GB of the 10 GB of storage. The body is a separate read, because together the two would bill about 145 GB, over the cap. (3) Test cheap: 2% samples of both models on the everyday dev target (6 jobs, 8 GiB) found two rule bugs. (4) Read: on October 31, on the full_scan target, post_history.text and then posts_questions.body. Comparing the stored original and current text question by question then found three more bugs in the rules for the original text, so post_history.text was read a second time. (5) Measure: the job log shows 511 GiB billed in all, 460 in October (45% of the month) and 50 in November (5%). The full_scan target ran 3 queries in the project's life.
    With an AI agent
    The code was written with an AI coding agent (Claude Code), so the limits had to hold without anyone reading every query first. They sit in five layers, from the spec to the shell:
    • Spec first. The cost rules are acceptance criteria, written before any SQL: the body is read once into a feature table and nothing downstream reads it (AC-3.2); the original text is read once and its size is stated (AC-3.4). The test assert_text_columns_read_once checks them on dbt's project graph, without reading a table: if anyone, agent or person, adds a model that reads a text column, the build fails before a byte is billed.
    • Caps that BigQuery enforces. Each dbt target carries maximum_bytes_billed (dev 20 GiB, full_scan 120 GiB per query): over it, BigQuery refuses the query, whoever wrote it. The default build skips the two scan models (selectors.yml); every EDA script dry-runs first and stops over 10 GB unless run with --allow-gb; the export script caps each query at 1 GiB; CI runs with no credentials at all.
    • A gate in the agent's shell. A local hook (machine-specific, kept out of the repo) checks every shell command before the agent runs it: it dry-runs a bq query and asks above 10 GB; it always asks before a dbt run, an mf query, an EDA run with --allow-gb or inline Python that touches BigQuery; when it can't estimate, it asks. Only a person can answer it. All three full reads were approved there, one at a time. The hook was tested with a 13-check script, including prompts declined in a live agent session.
    • A hook, not an instruction. A line in the agent's instructions, or a skill, is advice the agent can misread or skip; the hook runs before every command, so it holds even when the agent gets it wrong. Instructions still carry the why: AGENTS.md tells any agent working on a clone to dry-run first, keep the default build and never read body or post_history.text outside the two scan models.
    • The same rules for language-model spend. The P3 labeling script counts tokens for free and prints the expected and worst-case cost before any paid run, refuses to run without --max-usd, and stops starting calls at that cap; no run was allowed more than US$ 10. Labeling the whole 5% sample would have cost US$ 400–550, so 30,000 questions were drawn from it instead. Total: US$ 10.65, logged call by call.
    Impact
    • P1–P3. One read feeds all three prompts: the original tags behind P1's and P2's rebuild, and the text P3 measures.
    • Everyday work stays cheap. Everything except the two scan models rebuilds on the dev target, where no query has billed more than 3.9 GiB; queries/q1.sql bills about 9 GB and q2.sql about 7 GB.
    • The budget held. 511 GiB in all, never more than 45% of a month's free quota, and nothing paid.
    Trade-offs
    • The original text was read twice. Matching original and current text question by question needs both full tables (a sample takes whole storage blocks, so two samples can't be matched), so the last three rule bugs only showed after the first full read: 212 GiB instead of 106.
    • Only 5% of the raw text is kept. It fits the 10 GB of storage, but every later step that needs the raw text, such as P3's quality labels, is limited to that sample.
    • dbt has no dry run. A dbt query can't be priced before it runs, so the cap is the real guard there, and the gate asks before every dbt run, however small: many prompts for cheap builds.
    • The gate protects only this machine. A clone gets the caps, the default build, the graph test and AGENTS.md, but not the approval prompt.
    • Rebuilding pays again. Sandbox tables expire after 60 days, and anyone rebuilding P3 from scratch pays the 141 GiB once.
    • Two months helped. The work crossed from October into November; inside one month it would have used half the quota (511 of 1,024 GiB): still under, with less room for a third read.

    Executive impact of the decision

    “What did the data cost, and could the AI agent have run up a bill?”

    Nothing, and no. The project ran on BigQuery's free tier, 1 TB of queries a month. Over four days it billed 511 GiB, never more than 45% of a month. Half of that was one decision: reading the original text of every question (D4), at 106 GiB a read.

    We proved that read was worth it with 12 GB of metadata first, designed it to run once, and ran it on purpose: three big queries in the project's life, each approved by a person.

    The agent couldn't overspend by accident: BigQuery refuses any query over its cap, the default build skips the expensive models, a test fails any new model that touches the text, and a gate in the agent's shell asks a person before anything that could cost.

    • “What would it cost on a paid project?” About US$ 3 at BigQuery's on-demand list price (US$ 6.25 per TiB); the text reads are US$ 1.50 of it. The habits matter more than the bill: the same caps and gates scale to a warehouse where a careless query costs real money.
    • “Why not work on a sample?” We did, to test the rules (2%, 8 GiB). But P3's claims are about every question, and P1 and P2's rebuild needs every question's original tags, so the final read had to be full.
    • “What stops the next person's agent?” The caps, the default build and the graph test travel with the repo, and AGENTS.md tells any agent the rules. The approval gate is local; on a team, the next step is BigQuery's own custom quotas per user and per day.

    Which tags get answers?

    In 2022, text processing and data wrangling win: jq, awk, sed, regex, dplyr, data.table.     Only tags with 385+ questions are ranked (D1): 1,162 of 42,627 tags, but 76% of question–tag links (a question counts once in each of its tags), plus 1,026 pairs; a niche tag under 385 can't appear, however good.     { 09 · prompt 3 }

    What else goes with an answer?

    With the tags taken out, the strongest quality is a worked example: a concrete input and the exact output the asker wants, both written out. 60.0% of those questions end with an accepted answer, against 42.8% without (+17.2 points, the same sign in every year 2018–2021). The strongest negative is a question about the asker's own setup (installs, versions, deploys): 32.2% against 47.1%. Among the rules, a code block goes with +11.8 points and an asker with 20+ earlier questions with +16.6; posting time and "thanks in advance" barely matter. The P1 pattern again: what gets accepted can be solved at someone else's desk.

    Rules on all 7.16 million questions of 2018–2021, measured on the text as first posted (D4: 9–17% of questions a year were first edited after the first answer; a code block is +11.8 on that text, +11.3 on today's); labels are one model's judgments (TypeSafe Jev) on 30,000 random questions. Correlation, not cause. Groups under 385 questions are not reported (D1's floor). README, section 3. The panel queries a snapshot of the dbt mart: each label level's accepted rate next to its reference level's. Try l.version = 'never_edited', then Run.

    { HOW WE KNOW }

    EvidenceEDA
    Effects must keep their sign in each of 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021: 37 of the 43 rule effects do, and the other 6 are within 0.2 points of zero. Inside the 20 largest tags they barely move (a code block: +11.8 → +11.3). Against the rules, a worked example keeps +15.5 among questions with the same code and length; image-only evidence falls to −1.7, and 46% of its "yes" labels have no image at all, so I don't lean on it.
    Guardtests
    The gold set is the reference models' labels with my check on the ties: I changed 0 of the 567 labels they agreed on, so Jev's kappa (0.71–0.98, mean 0.80) is optimistic. The only blind check is the practice round, 20 questions labeled by hand before any model. Label noise pulls effects towards zero, so they are probably understated. A graph test fails if any model but the scans reads the text; a warn test comparing original and current text raises one warning.

Labeling 30,000 questions with Jev: US$ 3.82 (about US$ 10.65 for every model call in the project).

    { 10 · quality and cost }

    Tests, caps, and what it cost

    Quality

    • 170

      dbt tests: 153 generic, 14 singular and 3 unit tests on hand-written rows. Each one names its acceptance criterion; TESTING.md maps every criterion to its test and says where a gap is.

    • warn

      is the severity of the leakage impact checks: they never fail the build, they list what moves on the original version (P1: 46 tags and 65 pairs; P2: 3 rows), and the README discusses each.

    • 0 credentials

      in CI. GitHub Actions runs dbt parse, mf validate-configs --skip-dw and the Python checks on every push, without touching the warehouse.

    Cost

    • 3 layers

      kept BigQuery in check. A byte cap per dbt target (dev 20 GiB, full_scan 120 GiB per query), a 10 GB dry-run limit in every EDA script, and a local coding-agent hook (kept out of the repo) that dry-runs each query, asks before a big one or any dbt run, and asks whenever it can't estimate.

    • 511 GiB

      billed in BigQuery for the whole project, by its own job log: 45% of October's free 1 TB, US$ 0. The text reads are 247 GiB of it (post_history.text twice, after a rule fix; posts_questions.body once); every other query reads metadata only. How it was planned: D5, slide 5.

    • US$ 10.65

      of language-model calls, logged call by call: US$ 3.86 Anthropic (out of US$ 100 of promotional credits), US$ 2.96 Vercel AI Gateway, US$ 3.83 TypeSafe Jev (from the logged tokens; each call rounds to almost nothing in the log). BigQuery ran in the free sandbox.

    { 11 · limits }

    What this does not show

    Limits

    • Association, not cause. A tag or a quality goes with answers; nothing here shows that adding it to a question would help.
    • 2022 is partial and censored. The 30-day window and accepted among answered are the checks, not a fix.
    • Survivorship. Deleted questions are missing, so every rate reads high, older years more, by an amount this data can't size.
    • Literal populations. "Only python" means exactly one tag, and dbt-only is 168 questions in 2.7 years: read its intervals, not its points.
    • Edits. Exposure and the original-version checks measure the leakage; they don't remove it.
    • Model labels. P3's quality labels come from a language model, scored against a gold set I reviewed starting from two other models' labels, so the agreement is optimistic (chapter 9).

    What I'd do next

    • CI with the warehouse. dbt build on every push needs a cloud identity on the runner and a byte budget per run; left out on purpose.
    • Orchestration. Dagster, once the source is live instead of frozen, with source freshness back in place of the snapshot test.
    • A blind gold set. Hand labels made without the models' suggestions, as the independent check on the labeler.
    • Labels on the whole text sample. About 422,000 questions instead of 30,000, roughly US$ 400–550 with the cheapest labelers.
    • Hosted dbt docs. The lineage graph online; it needs a warehouse connection for the catalog.
